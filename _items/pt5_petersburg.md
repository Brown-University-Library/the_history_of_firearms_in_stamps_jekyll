Congress in July 1862 authorized the enlistment of “persons of African descent” in the military, thereby returning to the policy that had been followed by most state militias during the American Revolution and before.

By the end of the war, the regiments of “United States Colored Troops” comprised about a seventh of the U.S. Army.

At Petersburg, these troops could have won the Civil War then and there, according to a plan by Gen. Ambrose Burnside, who was a great supporter of them. Unfortunately, his plan was overridden by General Meade, who insisted that the decisive charge be made by White troops, who happened to be led by a notoriously unreliable officer. As a result, the siege of Petersburg, and the war, dragged on until April 1865.

Second only to federally-manufactured rifles, the British-made [1853 Enfield Rifle Musket](https://www.nramuseum.org/guns/the-galleries/a-nation-asunder-1861-to-1865/case-17-civil-war-imported-arms/barnett-model-1853-enfield-percussion-rifle-musket.aspx) was the most common firearm on both sides of the Civil War.
