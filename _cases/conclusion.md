Stamps are one way for a nation’s government to tell national stories to a nation’s public, and also to people in other countries who receive mail from that nation. The prime message conveyed by the American firearms stamps is not about American firearms, but about the American people, portraying them as active defenders of their liberties and energetic settlers of the continent. To the extent that foes are portrayed, they are never shown in a negative light, but instead presented as valiant warriors, such as the British, the Confederates, and Native Americans. 

Sometimes, individual stamps are conceived as part of a series, such as the Bicentennial stamps or the "Celebrate the Century" panes. Occasionally, stamps consciously look back to much earlier stamps, as in the 1998 reissue of the 1898 westward expansion stamps, the Bicentennial's new versions of American Revolution stamps from the Sesquicentennial, or the America 250 “Battlefields” stamps building on the the Sesquicentennial and the Bicentennial. Another story appears when stamps are viewed, as in most of this exhibit, in chronological order of the events depicted, rather than in order of when they were issued. 

Chronologically, the American firearms stamps tell a story of continual progress, beginning with primitive European matchlocks giving way to the more reliable flintlocks, which were better suited for the conditions in America. In the nineteenth century, the development of mass production using interchangeable parts hastened the pace of innovation, and inventions such as the percussion cap and smokeless powder facilitated major improvements in design. Among the fruits of invention were revolvers starting in the 1830s, plus breechloaders and lever-action repeaters in the 1850s. Before the end of the nineteenth century, pump-action shotguns, bolt-action rifles, and semiautomatic rifles appeared on the stage, and all of them were widely used in the twentieth century. 

The Bicentennial decade of the 1970s, and its many stamps related to the American Revolution, marked the greatest period of stamps depicting firearms. More broadly, firearms stamps were common in the last three quarters of the twentieth century and the twenty-first, rare in the nineteenth, and did not appear in the twenty-first until the nation’s 250th anniversary, in 2026. 

Some things have appeared on American stamps more often than firearms—for example: horses, flowers, or flags. Nevertheless, no other nation has produced so many firearms stamps, and the plenitude of firearms on American stamps reflects the importance of firearms in American history and culture.
